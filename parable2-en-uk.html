The Parable of Ananta's Slaying and the Great Self-Deception

Journey to Transparency of Perception

There was on Vaikuṇṭha that time of deep autumn when time stands still in transparent expectation. The night was not merely the absence of day, but a living, breathing substance through which the very fabric of creation showed itself. The sky, devoid of even a hint of clouds, revealed to the gaze not just stars—but the spiral arms of galaxies, nebulae shining like divine thoughts, and star clusters hanging like garlands of nectar droplets. The air was cold and pure to such an extent that each breath became an act of purification, filling the being with crystalline clarity. And over all this reigned an enormous, incredibly bright white moon, frozen at the very horizon. It did not move for several hours, emitting not a soft light, but a dazzling, almost merciless radiance, casting shadows sharp as blades and turning sapphire lakes into mirrors reflecting the entire universe at once.

Under the tree of Kalpavṛkṣa, whose branches stretched to the very other galaxies, sat Bhagavān Viṣṇu. His blue skin, the color of a thundercloud, absorbed the cosmic light, and in his eyes, calm and bottomless, whole worlds floated. Beside Him, as His own coming shadow, dwelt Mahā Viṣṇu—the future incarnation, from whose womb universes are born.

Bhagavān Viṣṇu: "O Revered One, Mahā Viṣṇu, You whose breath will become prana for new worlds. To You I address my question, for only He who is my coming future can know the causes of wounds inflicted upon my eternal 'now.' Listen. Seven thousand years ago, in the lands now called Rus', something irreparable occurred. By the force of ignorance, born from unconscious breathing, the great serpent Ananta-Śeṣa was slain—not his body, for it is eternal, but his very idea, his essence as the support of the universe. How could it happen that a roe deer drinking from a stream decided it could drink the entire ocean?"
Mahā Viṣṇu: "O Preserver, Bhagavān, the cause lies in the forgotten practice—Bhagavān Kriyā. When the impulse of inhalation, affirming 'I am,' and the impulse of exhalation, striving for unity, ceased to be consciously directed against one another to attain equilibrium, the ego of humans rose up like dry wood ready for fire. They could no longer distinguish the support of the world from its trial. By slaying Ananta in their perception, they did not destroy him, but distorted him. His multi-headed image, the symbol of infinite service, disintegrated in their reality into many ugly forms—serpent-dragons, asps stinging their own tails. They began to fight with the shadow they themselves had cast."
Bhagavān Viṣṇu: "But what fruits has this battle with the wind borne?"
Mahā Viṣṇu: "The bitterest, O Lord. Having lost the Vedic wisdom (Vaidika Jñāna), which was the reflection of Ananta in their hearts, they lost the knowledge of the Single Ātman. To drown the pain of this loss, the voice of inner emptiness, they found another drink—'fire-water.' It became a false pranayama, a perverted Bhagavān Kriyā, where inhalation and exhalation merged not in harmony, but in fiery chaos of self-destruction. Over seven thousand years, this poison has claimed the lives of fifty billion of their souls. This is not war, oh no. This is—the slow suicide of an entire culture that forgot that the source of life is in the pause between inhalation and exhalation."

The gaze of Bhagavān Viṣṇu became even deeper; in it flashed the sadness of all the fathers of the world.

Bhagavān Viṣṇu: "And is there no salvation for them? Is the chain of consequences unbreakable?"
Mahā Viṣṇu: "Salvation lies in the same place as the fall—in their perception. All themes must be considered in the context of transparency of perception. Until they see that the serpent-dragon is not an external enemy, but their own untamed ego, grown into a thousand-headed hydra of desires and fears, the battle will continue. They try to make peace with other countries, but, as you know, without resolving the root causes of contradictions within the countries themselves, within each heart, peace will remain inaccessible. They must destroy all harmful technologies and ideas that obscure the practice of breathing—that very Bhagavān Kriyā that leads to the neutralization of ego. They need to remember that Ananta was not slain. He merely coiled up, waiting for their consciousness to become pure enough again to serve as support for their vanishing race."
Bhagavān Viṣṇu: "Tell me more about the mechanism of this great self-deception. How did it happen that the war with the serpent became merely a pretext for its eternal existence?"
Mahā Viṣṇu: "It is the subtlest play of ego, O Preserver. By slaying the idea of Ananta-Śeṣa as cosmic support, or more precisely, the intuitive vision of the entire Cosmology in the cyclicity of Yugas, people preserved his image but endowed it with the opposite meaning—that of the absolute enemy. Thus was born the myth of the Serpent-Dragon, with whom one must fight. But this battle became for them not a goal, but a justification. They proclaim war on the serpent-alcohol, create laws against it, glorify heroes who defeat it, as on that coat of arms of the city of Moscow, where a horseman strikes a serpent. But all this formal war is merely an illusion, a ritual that allows the illusion of the enemy to remain, and even rule, in their lives.

They fight against alcohol, prohibiting its unrestrained mass production, but in secret they desire it. They defeat the dragon in paintings and tales, but in life they worship it. This 'war' has become for them a sacred ritual, without which the consumption of the poison would lose its taste as forbidden fruit. They do not wish to defeat the serpent—they wish for the battle with it to last forever, for it gives their ego justification for existence, a meaning of struggle that replaces for them the true meaning of life—the cognition of Ātman in the intuitive comprehension of the entire spiritual Cosmology, embodied in the wisdom of serving Ananta-Śeṣa to you, my dear, O Bhagavān Viṣṇu!

And therefore, O Bhagavān, every time they proclaim a new 'battle' with the green serpent, its consumption only increases. Every prohibition gives birth to a new, more sophisticated desire. They fight with a shadow so as not to see that the real enemy is within. This is the greatest illusion—formal war as a way of preserving vice."
Bhagavān Viṣṇu: "And how to break this circle?"
Mahā Viṣṇu: "Only through transparency of perception, leading to the resolution of all contradictions of Ego. They must realize the mechanism of the Ego's work, must see that the horseman and the serpent on the coat of arms are not two different entities, but two aspects of one consciousness. That the struggle with the serpent-alcohol is a struggle with a part of themselves. And until they accept this shadow, do not recognize it as a distorted need for union with the Absolute, they will forever circle in this dance of death.

They need to return to the culture of breathing—to Bhagavān Kriyā. Consciously directing the impulses of inhalation and exhalation against each other, they will attain that very pause, that silence, where all contradictions disappear. Where the horseman and the serpent understand that they are one. Where struggle loses meaning, for division disappears. Only then will the harmful technology of self-deception be destroyed, and they will remember the taste of the true nectar—the amrita of unity."

And at that moment, the nature of Vaikuṇṭha attained completion. The enormous white moon finally stirred and began its unhurried path upward, its light softened, became more merciful. In the waters of the lakes washing the roots of Kalpavṛkṣa, appeared those very first mirrors of awareness. In them was visible everything: the warrior on a red horse, striking a multi-headed dragon with a spear, and the dragon itself, in whose eyes was the same pain as in the warrior's eyes; and the man bringing a cup of fire-water to his lips, and his very soul, languishing with thirst for genuine nectar; and all those formal wars with the serpent that were merely a cover for eternal devotion to it. These contradictions did not argue, but coexisted, showing that darkness is merely the absence of light, and evil is distorted good. The transparency of perception dissolved all conflicts, returning them to the single source—to the silence where dwells the primordial, unborn 'I'.
